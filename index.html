Rising Tides: how the coastline changes, 1900–2100

As the world warms, oceans expand and glaciers and ice sheets melt, so sea level rises. This map shows which land along the coast ends up below the water line in any year from 1900 to 2100, depending on how much the planet warms. Pick a country, move the sliders, and watch which cities and towns are affected.

What is the issue?

Global average sea level has risen about 20 cm since 1900, and the rise is speeding up: about 1.5 mm per year over the 20th century, and about 3–4 mm per year since satellites began measuring in 1993. Two things cause it: seawater expands as it warms, and glaciers and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets melt and add water to the ocean. The more the world warms, the faster the sea rises.

Hundreds of millions of people live within a few metres of sea level. A coastal mayor, a planner choosing where to build, or a family deciding where to buy a house needs to know: which places end up under water, and how much does that depend on how much we warm the planet?

What do the sliders do?

  • Year (1900–2100): moves through time. Before 2020 you see what was actually measured; the coast in 1900 was about 20 cm further out than today (orange areas).
  • Warming by 2100 (−5 to +5 °C): how hot the world gets by the end of the century. More warming means a faster and larger rise. Negative values are a "what if the world cooled" thought experiment; they show the sea falling.
  • Storm surge (optional, 0–5 m): water piled up temporarily by a storm and high tide, on top of the average sea level.

What should you notice?

  • The sliders depend on each other. Before 2020 the warming slider does nothing, because the past is already fixed. The later the year, the more the warming matters: in 2030 the gap between 1.4 °C and 4.4 °C is only a few centimetres, but by 2100 it is about 40 cm.
  • Each extra degree adds roughly 13 cm by 2100, and the rise does not stop in 2100. Over the following centuries, each °C commits the world to about 2.3 m more (Levermann et al., 2013).
  • Small rises matter most when storms hit. Set surge to 2 m and compare 1900 with 2100: the same storm floods noticeably more land once the sea is higher.
  • Places to try: Bangladesh, Vietnam (Mekong Delta), Egypt (Nile Delta), the Netherlands, and the United States (zoom to the Gulf Coast and Florida).

How is it calculated?

1900–2020: global mean sea level from tide-gauge and satellite reconstructions (Frederikse et al., 2020; NASA), about +0.18 m from 1900 to 2018.

2020–2100: the rise by 2100 (relative to 1995–2014) is a straight-line fit to the five IPCC AR6 scenarios: rise₂₁₀₀ = 0.198 + 0.130 × T metres, where T is the warming in °C. Check: T = 2.7 °C gives 0.198 + 0.351 = 0.55 m; the IPCC median for SSP2-4.5 is 0.56 m. Between 2020 and 2100 the curve speeds up gradually: SL(t) = SL₂₀₂₀ + (rise₂₁₀₀ − SL₂₀₂₀) × (0.5τ + 0.5τ²), with τ = (year − 2020)/80.

Map: ground height comes from the open Terrain Tiles elevation dataset (SRTM, GMTED, ETOPO and other sources). A pixel is shaded blue if its height is above 0 m (dry today) but below the water level. This is called a "bathtub" model. Flooding is drawn in squares that are always 4×4 screen pixels; each square's shade shows the fraction of its land that floods (lighter blue = partly flooded). Zoomed out, a square covers kilometres and uses the coarser elevation data for that zoom; zoom in for squares of a few tens of metres. Because the coarse and fine elevation data come from different sources, small details can shift slightly between zoom levels. When zoomed out, each elevation pixel's height is an average over kilometres, which hides narrow strips of low coast and shallow sea floor. For pixels right on the coastline the site therefore assumes the ground rises evenly from the shoreline across the pixel: a coastal pixel with average height h has a share L / (2h) of its area below water level L (and likewise for shallow sea floor when the sea falls). That is why coasts show faint colour when zoomed out; zoom in for the measured result.

One city: every elevation pixel within a circle around the city centre is counted. The circle's radius grows with population (1.5 km × √(population / 100,000), between 2 and 25 km). The share of land pixels at or below the water level is the share of the city under water.

Limitations: read before you trust a number

  • Global average only. Real local sea level also depends on land sinking or rising (for example, parts of the US Gulf Coast are sinking), on ocean currents and on gravity effects of melting ice. Local rise can be much larger or smaller than the global number.
  • No sea walls or levees. Protected places like the Netherlands or New Orleans show up as flooded even though defences keep the water out today. The model also does not check whether low inland land is actually connected to the sea.
  • Zoomed-out shading is an estimate. At wide views, flooding along coastlines is estimated by assuming the ground rises evenly away from the shore inside each kilometres-wide pixel. Real coasts with cliffs or wide flat marshes will differ; zoom in to see the measured result.
  • Elevation data is coarse. Satellite elevation data can be off by several metres, which is larger than the sea-level change itself. When zoomed out, each pixel covers kilometres. Treat the map as an illustration, not a property-level flood map.
  • Ice-sheet uncertainty. The IPCC cannot rule out a rise approaching 2 m by 2100 under very high emissions, if the Antarctic ice sheet collapses faster than expected. Those low-likelihood outcomes are not included here.
  • Outside the IPCC scenarios the line is extrapolated. Warming below about 1.4 °C or above 4.4 °C is not covered by the IPCC scenarios, and cooling below pre-industrial (negative values) would require removing more than all the extra greenhouse gas humans have added. Those settings show the direction of change, not a forecast.
